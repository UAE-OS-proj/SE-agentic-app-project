Scrum is an agile framework for developing and delivering complex products in small, incremental steps. Instead of planning everything upfront like the Waterfall model, a Scrum team works in short cycles called sprints, usually one to four weeks long. At the end of every sprint the team produces a usable increment of the product, which gets feedback from stakeholders and shapes the next sprint.

Scrum defines three roles. The Product Owner is responsible for the product backlog, which is the ordered list of everything the product might need. The owner decides priorities and makes sure the team understands what delivers the most value. The Scrum Master is a servant leader who coaches the team on Scrum practices, removes blockers, and protects the team from outside interruptions. The Developers are the people who do the work of building, testing, and delivering the increment. Together these roles form the Scrum Team.

Scrum has five events. The Sprint is the container event that holds all the others. Sprint Planning is where the team chooses which backlog items to work on and defines a sprint goal. The Daily Scrum is a short meeting of up to 15 minutes where developers sync on progress and obstacles. The Sprint Review is held at the end of the sprint, where the team demonstrates the increment and gathers feedback. The Sprint Retrospective follows the review, and the team reflects on how it worked and agrees on improvements for the next sprint.

Scrum also has three artifacts. The Product Backlog is the single source of requirements for the product. The Sprint Backlog is the set of items selected for the current sprint, plus the plan for delivering them. The Increment is the sum of all completed backlog items, and it must meet the Definition of Done, a shared checklist that says when work is considered finished. Teams often track progress with a burndown chart, which shows the remaining work in a sprint over time.

Scrum is built on three pillars: transparency, inspection, and adaptation. Work and progress must be visible to everyone involved, the team regularly inspects its product and process, and it adapts when something is not working. Compared with the Waterfall model, Scrum handles changing requirements better, delivers working software earlier, and gives customers frequent chances to give feedback.
